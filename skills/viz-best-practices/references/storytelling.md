# Storytelling, Context & Narrative

Primarily Knaflic (the six lessons, narrative structure), with Dougherty's "tell-show-why" and Wilke's "figure for the generals."

---

## 1. Understand the context FIRST (before touching a chart)

**Exploratory vs explanatory:** Exploratory = opening 100 oysters to find 2 pearls. Explanatory = communicating those 2 pearls. When *communicating*, you're in the explanatory space — don't make the audience re-open all the oysters. Resist showing everything as "proof of work."

**Who / What / How (in this order):**
- **Who** — be specific about the audience. Avoid "general audiences" — you can't communicate to everyone at once. Identify the decision-maker.
- **What** — what do you need them to **know or do**? Always have an ask. If you only present data, they say "interesting" and move on. Take a confident stance and recommend an action.
- **How** — the mechanism, on a continuum from **live presentation** (you control pace; sparse slides; don't use slides as a teleprompter) to **written document** (audience controls; needs more detail). The "slideument" serving both satisfies neither.

**Tools to boil down the message:**
- **3-minute story** — if you had only 3 minutes, what would you say?
- **Big Idea (Duarte)** — one sentence: your unique POV + what's at stake + a complete sentence.
- **Storyboard** — the single most important up-front step. Start **low-tech** (whiteboard, Post-its) so you don't get attached to content you should cut. Don't start in presentation software.

Don't hide non-supporting data — a discerning audience will find it and discredit you.

## 2. Tell a story — narrative structure

Stories stick where facts alone don't (McKee: people aren't inspired to act by reason alone; story unites idea + emotion). Story = how/why things change: balance → an event upsets it → struggle → resolution.

**Three-act structure (Aristotle / setup-conflict-resolution):**
- **Beginning** — set up the context, the main character (**frame the audience as the hero**), the imbalance, the desired state. Answer "why should I care / what's in it for me?" Conflict/tension is essential — "the conflict between what is and what could be" (Duarte).
- **Middle** — develop "what could be": background, comparisons, examples, the cost of inaction, options, benefits of your recommendation, why the audience is positioned to act.
- **End** — **end with a call to action.** Make crystal clear what you want them to do. Tie back to the opening.

**Narrative flow / order:**
- **Chronological** (problem → analysis → recommendation) — good for establishing credibility or when process matters.
- **Lead with the ending** (call to action first, then support) — good when trust exists or they just want the "so what." Bonus: tells them the lens upfront.

**Repetition** moves info to long-term memory. **Bing-Bang-Bongo:** tell them what you'll tell them → tell them → recap.

**Checks the story is clear:**
- **Horizontal logic** — reading just the slide titles in order tells the whole story (requires action titles).
- **Vertical logic** — everything on a slide reinforces the title and each other; nothing extraneous.
- **Reverse storyboard** — flip through and write each page's main point; it should read like your intended storyboard.
- **Fresh eyes** — give it to someone without context; ask what they notice, find important, and question.

## 3. Tell — Show — Why (Dougherty)

Data visualizations don't speak for themselves — interpret them for the reader.
- **Tell** the audience what you found, **Show** the visual evidence, say **Why** it matters.
- One-sentence insight at the **top** of each viz; why-it-matters at the **bottom**.
- State the question **before** the answer; summarize each insight **before** showing its evidence.
- "Describe the forest, not every tree — but point out a few special trees."
- Build credibility: represent data truthfully, cite sources, show your work, disclose uncertainty and limitations.

---

## 4. Make a figure "for the generals" (Wilke)

- Assume the audience is busy, won't decipher complexity, and won't infer your point. **Less is more** — simplify ruthlessly, drop dimensions tangential to the story. A clear bar chart beats an impressive multi-variable scatter. *Never assume the audience can rapidly process complex visual displays.*
- **Build up to complexity** — show one simple subplot before the full small-multiples grid, especially live.
- **Make figures memorable** (for broad audiences) — but weight toward clarity for technical audiences. Use images to *represent* data, not to decorate.

---

## 5. Resonance & honesty (Steele & Iliinsky)

- *Question + Visual Data + Context = Story.* Data is meaningful only in context — provide it up front so the viewer doesn't have to hold it in their head.
- A great visualization **draws you in and invites exploration** — "a line graph can illustrate a fact, but it rarely incites you to probe further." Get the resonance right and viewers forgive small warts.
- But the **journalist over storyteller** caution (Odewahn): weave facts objectively rather than invent a narrative to fit a plot. Fidelity to the underlying facts is what makes it beautiful.
- And: **a clear message is not necessarily a true one.** Clarity persuades; persuasion is sales — so always ask *why* you're seeing what's in front of you.

---

## 6. Animation — when it helps (Fisher, Steele & Iliinsky)

"If a 2D image is good, a moving image is better" is **wrong.** Across ~100 studies animation never beat rich static diagrams for process comprehension; for unfamiliar data it was slower AND less accurate than small multiples / tracked views.
- Rosling's famous talk works because it's **presentation** (he knows the answer, narrates, guides the gaze, consistent axes) — not exploration.
- **Use animation to transition between views** to keep users oriented. **Don't animate when users must compare before vs after** — switch atomically.
- If you must animate: do **one thing at a time**, animate **only what changes**, keep each **frame meaningful** (for print/share), preserve valid data→visual mappings throughout.

---

## 7. Make it pretty — and get it accepted (Knaflic)

- **Aesthetics matter:** people perceive prettier designs as easier to use, are more tolerant of flaws, and engage longer. Pretty buys patience for your message.
- Three aesthetic levers: smart (sparing) color, clean alignment, generous white space.
- **Acceptance:** people resist the new. Articulate the benefits, show a side-by-side before/after, offer options and seek input, get an influential person on board first.
- **Iterate:** start on paper; use the "optometrist approach" (change one thing, compare A vs B); seek fresh eyes. Budget real time for the communication step — it's the only part the audience ever sees.
